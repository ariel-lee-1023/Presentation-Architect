# slide:ology: The Art and Science of Creating Great Presentations — Nancy Duarte
**Format**: Markdown with PDF metadata and selected visual checks | **Pages**: 276 PDF pages | **Sections**: 12 | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
A visual slide is a designed relationship between an idea and what an audience can perceive. Generate and sketch alternatives before selecting assets; then make position, scale, grouping, color and motion express the intended meaning. This book's main bridge is idea → visual concept → slide, distinct from Resonate's audience journey. It primarily addresses speaker-led presentations and explicitly distinguishes them from documents.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1. Creating a New Slide Ideology — identify the communication medium
Source: chapter 1, pp. 6–7, “So Where Do You Begin?” and “The Presentation Ecosystem.”

- **Document / Teleprompter / Presentation**: distinguish material meant to be read, text that acts as a speaker's script, and visual support for a live message. Dense submissions can be legitimate discussion documents. Do not pretend that projecting a document while reading it aloud creates an effective presentation.
- **Slideument**: a hybrid that often serves neither reading nor live delivery well. Decide its use explicitly; distribute a real reading artifact when necessary. A hybrid can be engineered through a projected layer plus notes or a companion document, rather than a compromise that makes both audiences work harder.
- **Presentation ecosystem**: message, visual story and delivery have to support one another. A slide may be polished yet fail because the speaker's claim or pacing does not fit it.
- **Audience needs**: identify who will view the work, what matters to them, why they came, what change is sought, how they might resist, and how the presenter can help. Treat an audience persona as a working model, not a substitute for actual context.
- **Density heuristics are contextual**: the book uses word-count examples to distinguish documents from projected support. They are diagnostics within this medium, not a rule that a page becomes bad at a particular word count.

### 2. Creating Ideas, Not Slides — make alternatives cheap
Source: chapter 2, “Finding Your Inspiration,” “Innovating with Sticky Notes,” “Sketching Your Way to Success,” pp. 26–41.

- **Generate ideas before assets**: use words, diagrams, scenes and associations before opening a layout. A stock image search too early can lock the message into the available picture. Produce several concepts, then judge them against the purpose.
- **Word-mapping**: explore associations around a concept to get beyond the first literal or clichéd metaphor. Test whether the chosen association actually preserves the relationship to be explained; resemblance alone is not an argument.
- **One idea per sticky note**: externalize units so they can be sorted, grouped, discarded and reordered. The note is a thinking constraint rather than a rule about final slide density.
- **Sketching as proof of concept**: rough layouts reveal whether the relationship can work spatially and within available production resources. Compare alternatives while revisions are cheap. A sketch can reject an idea because it misdirects attention even before it looks polished.
- **Collaborative clarity**: walk colleagues through the sketches and ask what they infer. The relevant feedback is whether the concept communicates, not simply which picture they prefer.
- **Scene or sequence**: some relationships need a single display; others require multiple states. Choose based on what must remain visible and what changes through time. Avoid using a sequence to hide comparison context.

### 3. Creating Diagrams — encode the relationship accurately
Source: chapter 3, pp. 44–61, especially “Classifying Diagrams” and “Strengthening the Diagram's Concept.”

The book groups diagrams into six families. Select by structure before styling:

| Family | Relationship | Construction and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Flow** | Movement, change or progression | Use linear, circular, divergent/convergent or multidirectional organization. An arrow must mean an actual direction or dependency. |
| **Structure** | Organization, containment or levels | Use matrices, trees or layers. Verify that subordinate placement represents real subordination. |
| **Cluster** | Association or membership | Use overlap, closure, enclosure or links. Proximity and overlap imply relationships; do not add them merely for balance. |
| **Radiate** | A relation to a center | Distinguish arrangements with or without a core. A large center claims centrality; ensure that claim is intended. |
| **Pictorial** | A concrete place, object or physical process | Use recognizable depiction for direction, location, reveal, process or influence. Extra realism is useful only where recognition or mechanism needs it. |
| **Display Data** | Comparison, trend or distribution | Encode quantities in an appropriate chart with preserved scales and context. A decorative schematic cannot replace measured quantities. |

- **Diagram families**: repeat a visual vocabulary across related concepts so the audience learns it once. Similar forms should imply similar relationships; consistency that obscures a real difference is harmful.
- **Strengthen the concept**: test whether the organizing relationship reflects the audience's concern. In the book's advertising example, recentering several diagrams on the client turns disconnected graphics into a coherent conceptual family.

### 4. Displaying Data — clarity and integrity travel together
Source: chapter 4, pp. 64–79, “Following the Five Data Slide Rules.”

**The Five Data Slide Rules** are **Tell the truth; Get to the point; Pick the right tool for the job; Highlight what's important; Keep it simple**.

- **Tell the truth**: preserve the meaning of the data and be ready to provide the complete dataset. Analytical audiences can reasonably distrust a display that strips out too much. Do not treat criticism of clutter as permission to remove qualifications.
- **Get to the point**: state what should become understandable after inspection. The intended inference helps select and annotate a display. It must remain an inference licensed by the evidence, not a desired conclusion imposed on it.
- **Pick the right tool**: a single meaningful number may need text rather than a chart; a comparison needs comparable marks. For complex inspection at a distance, provide a handout rather than shrinking the evidence until it is unreadable.
- **Highlight what's important**: accent the relevant time, region or series while preserving the surrounding context. The HR-training illustration highlights the timing of training in a sales curve. Timing shows association; Presentation Architect must not repeat this as proof that the training caused the change.
- **Keep it simple**: reduce representational overhead and ornament. Maintain what is needed to judge uncertainty, magnitude and alternatives. Multiple small aligned charts can be clearer than a decorative 3D display.
- **Stable axes**: align successive charts so the coordinate frame does not jump. For actual comparisons also check scale, units and time period; stable placement alone does not establish comparability.

### 5–6. Thinking Like a Designer; Arranging Elements — make attention express meaning
Source: chapters 5–6, especially “Placement of Elements Creates Meaning,” pp. 92–111.

- **Design decisions express intent**: choose the emotional and conceptual character appropriate to the subject. Communicating seriousness does not require generic corporate decoration; communicating warmth does not justify ambiguity in evidence.
- **Contrast** identifies the main point through differences of size, shape, shade, color or location. Select the dominant element rather than making every element loud.
- **Flow** organizes how attention moves through a composition. Establish a readable entry and progression, then check whether a viewer sees the evidence before the conclusion that depends on it.
- **Hierarchy** expresses relationships of importance, parenthood and subordination. Size and proximity alter meaning; making a supporting bullet heavier than the title can invert the argument.
- **Unity** makes elements belong through grid, style and theme. Use recurring anchors so comparable content is easy to find and does not jump between slides.
- **Proximity** groups related material. Keep a label with the thing it describes, and separate independent groups enough to avoid false association.
- **Whitespace** supplies breathing room and separation. Space is functional when it clarifies relationships or emphasis; unoccupied space is not automatically a superior design.
- **Flexible grid**: allocate regions for text, charts and images and test different compositions within them. The grid coordinates elements; it does not require each slide to contain the same boxes.

### 7. Background, Color, and Text — establish the visual grammar
Source: chapter 7, “The Ingredients of a Great Slide,” pp. 114–155; typesetting pp. 146–149.

- **Four visual ingredients**: background, color, text and images. Make a few coherent decisions and carry them through the deck. A rare intentional break can emphasize a turning point; constant exceptions remove the signal.
- **Background as surface**: its purpose is to hold the message visibly. A texture, logo or dimensional effect must earn its space. Check whether it competes with evidence or labels.
- **Color as emphasis and identity**: define consistent roles rather than choosing new colors independently on each slide. Meaning, contrast and viewing conditions matter more than a fashionable palette. Avoid making color the only way to distinguish essential categories.
- **Text as an element**: size, weight and position express hierarchy before a word is read. Keep terms and levels consistent. Cut unnecessary words before shrinking a necessary explanation.
- **Kerning** changes space between particular letter pairs; **tracking/letterspacing** changes spacing across a run. Inspect display titles where awkward gaps become conspicuous. Do not use tracking to squeeze long passages into inadequate space.
- **Ligatures** combine letterforms where the typeface and writing system support them. They improve fit in specific cases; they are not a universal decorative treatment.
- **Line versus paragraph spacing**: separate lines within one thought from gaps between thoughts. A subpoint should sit closer to its parent than to the next independent group.
- **Font-size validation**: judge the actual screen, distance, contrast and content. The source's projector-era heuristics do not remove the need to inspect current delivery conditions.

### 8. Images — build an image system
Source: chapter 8, “Assembling an Image System,” “Taking Your Own Photos,” “Illustrations Using the Personality of Lines,” “Illustrating Complex Stories.”

- **Image system**: choose a coherent photographic or illustrative treatment so a deck reads as one work. Define cropping, background, color treatment and placement. Images should reveal an idea, support evidence or establish relevant atmosphere.
- **Literal versus metaphorical**: literal images establish recognizable objects and contexts; metaphors transfer a relationship. Test what the metaphor implies and where it breaks. A handshake image usually does less explanatory work than a diagram of the actual partnership.
- **Illustration** can make hidden mechanisms and abstract relations visible. Simplify rendering detail while preserving the mechanism. Distinguish a conceptual illustration from a factual image.
- **Human context**: including people can clarify who acts or benefits. It can also wrongly shift attention away from the relevant system. Judge it against the slide's function rather than a rule to humanize every page.

### 9. Creating Movement — design time deliberately
Source: chapter 9, “Designing Time-Based Scenes,” “Planning Animations,” “Animating Serves a Purpose,” pp. 180–201.

- **Time-based scenes**: movement changes the audience's focus. Use it to reveal a transition, preserve an object's identity across states, or coordinate explanation with inspection.
- **Purposeful animation**: decide what becomes clearer because something moves. Unmotivated swooshes and transitions demand attention without contributing meaning.
- **Scenes, not isolated slides**: related frames can form one continuous explanation. Preserve reference points across changes so the viewer can follow the transformation.
- **Avoid visual vertigo**: excessive movement and abrupt spatial changes impair orientation. Provide a static final state, and ensure that distributed versions retain the explanation without animation.

### 10–11. Governing with Templates; Interacting with Slides
Source: chapters 10–11, pp. 203–249, including “Making Template Design Decisions,” “Constraining the Projector” and the contrasting slide-count discussions.

- **Template as system**: masters should define reusable layout families, anchors, type and color roles. A template is successful when varied content can remain coherent; a decorative frame that leaves no room for evidence is unsuccessful.
- **Collaborative production**: establish shared conventions before many authors assemble slides. Review the combined argument and terminology after merging; visual consistency alone cannot reconcile contradictory claims.
- **Constraints**: time, audience, technology and distribution affect design choices. Constraining length can improve selection, but arbitrary slide limits should not fragment a necessary display.
- **Different slide-count prescriptions**: the chapter juxtaposes the 10/20/30 rule, very large slide counts and technology-dependent pacing. Their coexistence prevents treating any one count as universal. Rehearse and time the actual presentation.
- **To project or not**: physical props, conversation or a board may better serve a moment. Choose the medium because it helps the audience understand, not because a projector is available.

### 12. Manifesto — design serves the audience
Source: chapter 12, “The Five Theses of the Power of a Presentation.”

The five theses are **Treat Your Audience as King; Spread Ideas and Move People; Help Them See What You're Saying; Practice Design, Not Decoration; Cultivate Healthy Relationships**. Together they locate the purpose of visual craft in communication and connection. They are ethical and practical commitments, not measures of a slide's statistical validity.

## Worked Example
In the software-development sketching example (pp. 38–39), Duarte compares ways to depict a management system. One sketch is rejected because it glorifies the system rather than how developers work. The retained concept includes employees who communicate and then work at their tasks while remaining connected through the management environment. The lesson is not “always add faces.” First state what the audience must understand about coordination; sketch alternative centers and connections; then inspect whether the diagram assigns importance to the right actors. Rendering comes after that decision. The example supports a representation principle, not a claim that this arrangement improves every organization's workflow.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- If the idea is still unclear, sketch competing representations before selecting an image or template.
- If the content expresses a relationship, choose a diagram family that encodes that relationship explicitly.
- If position, size or overlap implies a claim you did not intend, change the composition.
- If a chart is needed for independent examination, retain context and provide a readable inspection medium.
- If the artifact is a document, design it for reading rather than enforcing projected-slide word counts.
- If emphasis suggests causality or certainty beyond the evidence, revise the claim and display.
- If motion has no explanatory purpose, remove it; if it has a purpose, preserve a usable static version.
- If template consistency prevents truthful representation, revise the layout family instead of distorting the information.

## Key Takeaways
1. Visual concepts should precede digital polish.
2. Diagram topology and visual hierarchy make assertions.
3. A coherent system permits varied layouts.
4. Data integrity comes before persuasive emphasis.
5. Choose slide density and pacing from the actual medium and audience.
